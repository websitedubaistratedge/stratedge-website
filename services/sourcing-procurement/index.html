Where does procurement cost usually hide?

In contracts that were never renegotiated, in specifications more demanding than the business needs, and in a supply base too fragmented to give any single supplier a reason to sharpen. None of these show up as a line item — they show up as a cost base that grew faster than revenue.

How do you cut cost without losing reliability?

By separating the two decisions. Cost work targets specification, volume consolidation and contract terms; reliability is protected by qualifying suppliers properly and building performance measures into the agreement. Cutting price without doing the second is how supply problems start.

What does supplier identification involve?

Setting criteria before looking, then searching against them. A supplier chosen because they were introduced first is a decision made by accident. Criteria cover capability, capacity, financial stability, compliance and cultural fit with how you operate.

What the work covers

The work starts with visibility: what the business actually buys, from whom, on what terms, and at what total cost. In most organisations that picture has never been assembled in one place, and assembling it is itself often the largest single finding.

From there, supplier identification widens the field against defined criteria. Contract negotiation converts that leverage into terms. Vendor management and supply chain work put a structure around the relationship — performance measures, review cadence, escalation — so value is held rather than won once and lost.

Cost optimisation runs throughout, but as an outcome of better structure rather than as pressure applied to prices.

Procurement as a capability, not an event

A one-off sourcing exercise delivers a saving that erodes. What holds value is a process the business can repeat: criteria, a qualified supplier pool, contracts with review points, and measures that show whether performance is holding.

Engagements are built to leave that behind, so the next category can be run by your team.
